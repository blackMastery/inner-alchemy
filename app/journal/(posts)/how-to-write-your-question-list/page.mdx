Of all the preparation for a session, this is the part that changes the outcome most — and the part
people most often leave until the night before.

Your list isn't paperwork. There's an hour in the middle of the day given to going through it question by
question, and the answers come back in your own voice. What you write is, quite literally, what the
session spends its deepest hour on.

## Write them down, in your own words

You'll get an intake form after booking that walks you through this, but the principle is simple: write
the questions out, and write them the way you'd actually say them.

We use your exact wording in the session. That's not a formality. "Why do I keep choosing people who
can't stay?" and "What is my pattern in relationships?" are not the same question, and they don't get the
same answer. The first one has your life in it. The second could belong to anyone.

So resist the urge to tidy them into something more presentable. The unpolished version is the useful one.

## How many

Most people bring ten to fifteen. That's a good target.

Fewer than about eight and you'll likely run out before the hour does. Many more than fifteen and the
list starts to sprawl, and you'll find you'd rather have gone deeper on six of them than skated across
twenty-five.

Order them, too — put the ones that actually matter at the top. If time runs short, it should run short
on the questions you added because you felt you ought to.

## What people usually ask about

The four areas most lists cover:

- **Life purpose and direction** — what you're for, what you're avoiding, what you'd do if you weren't
  afraid of it.
- **Relationships** — the recurring shape of them, not just the current one.
- **Recurring patterns** — anything that has happened more than twice and that you can't trace the origin
  of. Money, work, conflict, the same argument in different clothes.
- **The body** — physical things you're carrying. Worth being specific here.

On that last one, one honest boundary: this work is complementary. It does not diagnose, treat, or cure
anything, and it is not a substitute for care from a doctor or a licensed therapist. Bring the questions
anyway — just keep your providers, and bring anything useful back into those conversations.

## The questions worth asking

A few things that tend to make a question better:

- **Ask about causes, not predictions.** "Where did this start?" reaches further than "will it work out?"
- **Ask the one you're slightly reluctant to write.** Almost everyone has a question they haven't put on
  the list because it feels too exposed, too petty, or too strange. It is very often the one the session
  turns on.
- **Ask about things you've already decided are just how you are.** The permanent-seeming traits. Those
  are frequently the most traceable.
- **Don't pre-answer them.** A question that contains its own conclusion — "why am I so bad at this?" —
  narrows what can come back. "What's happening when I try this?" leaves the door open.

## Bring the list with you

Print it or write it out; don't rely on scrolling a phone while deeply relaxed. Bring it even if you
think you'll remember. You won't want to be reconstructing it from memory on the day.

And then let it go until the session. The list's job is to be ready when we get there — not to be
rehearsed.
